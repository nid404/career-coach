# Organisational Politics: Beware the Simple Story
https://commoncog.com/organisational-politics-beware-the-simple-story/

By Cedric Chin

## The Problem of First Impressions

The author introduces three characters who struggle with accurate judgment: Bob, a brilliant programmer who forms rigid first impressions of colleagues; Jess, who generates consistently negative narratives about every workplace; and himself, acknowledging the fundamental attribution error where we blame others' mistakes on their character while excusing our own as circumstantial.

## Why Our Brains Create Simple Stories

The human brain functions as a "narrative machine" that readily accepts compelling stories as fact. When we observe behaviors, our brain generates explanations and accepts the first plausible narrative, then "shuts down the machinery that is responsible for analysis." This automatic process explains how we develop inaccurate character judgments and perpetuate them through confirmation bias, the narrative fallacy, primacy effects, and belief persistence.

## The Career Impact of Character Misjudgment

Accurate character assessment provides significant professional advantages. Conversely, poor judgment limits opportunities. Bob couldn't work effectively with people he'd already labeled "lazy" or "careless," constraining his team's composition. Jess's pattern of negative narratives about every workplace suggests self-generated dysfunction rather than genuine workplace toxicity.

## Solutions for Avoiding Judgment Errors

The author recommends generating "multiple narratives at the same time" rather than committing to the first compelling explanation. This aligns with Cognitive Behavioral Therapy principles—recognizing that thoughts and reality differ. Additionally, fostering environments encouraging productive disagreement, as Jonathan Haidt suggests, helps counteract individual bias.

## Correcting Others' Misjudgments

When others form erroneous judgments about you, timely action is critical. The author outlines a three-step process: (1) identify the triggering event using labeling techniques, (2) address the judgment directly without implying error, and (3) provide sporadic evidence contradicting their assessment. Success requires "discontinuous inconsistency" with their original judgment—small actions typically get dismissed.

Human nature remains constant across generations, making interpersonal skills genuinely learnable throughout one's lifetime.
