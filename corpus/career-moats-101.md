# Career Moats 101
https://commoncog.com/career-moats-101/

By Cedric Chin

## Introduction

Commoncog focuses on building career moats—a concept the author describes as central to his professional philosophy. This article summarizes key ideas about establishing competitive advantages in your career.

## What is a Career Moat?

A career moat represents "an individual's ability to maintain competitive advantages over your competition (say, in the job market)" to protect long-term prospects and employability. Rather than relying on keeping your current position, job security depends on your capacity to land your next role.

The author argues career moats matter because industries constantly evolve. Automation and globalization mean traditional career guarantees have disappeared. Building a moat provides peace of mind and strategic flexibility—the author used his own moat to confidently leave employment to pursue entrepreneurial ventures.

## What Comprises a Career Moat?

Career moats consist of rare and valuable skills. Critically, anything taught in standard coursework remains valuable but not rare, making degrees necessary but insufficient for building a moat.

The author identifies multiple pathways:

**Being exceptional at one skill:** Examples include Jeff Dean's distributed systems expertise at Google—success depends on maintaining dominance in a valuable domain.

**Possessing rare skill combinations:** Three approaches emerge:
- Opaque learning paths creating unique expertise
- Unattractive but valuable specializations (like COBOL programming)
- Early specialization before fields gain recognized importance

## Strategic Inflection Points

Drawing from Andy Grove's framework, the author suggests continuously monitoring emerging threats and opportunities:

- Avoid dismissing early-stage technologies
- Analyze how changes affect supplier, customer, and competitive relationships
- Systematically track observations over time

## Building a Career Moat

Practical approaches include pursuing "rigorous, informed trial and error" where tacit knowledge dominates, maintaining deep work capacity, building sustainable habits, and practicing consistently. The author emphasizes that practice determines success since explicit knowledge alone proves insufficient.

Key recommendations: read strategically for industry context, treat career moves as calculated bets, extract skills from experienced mentors through narrative analysis, and verify all advice through personal experience rather than accepting claims uncritically.
