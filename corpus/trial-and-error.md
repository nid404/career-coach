## Introduction

The author reflects on a Buzzfeed video where staffers attempted to make mozzarella from scratch, noting how their repeated failures and adjustments ultimately led to success. This observation connects to financial theorist Nicholas Nassim Taleb's argument about trial and error as a superior strategy.

## When Trial & Error Works Best

Taleb proposes that trial and error excels under two conditions: when randomness exists and when outcomes exhibit convexity—meaning positive results outweigh negative ones. The mozzarella-making example illustrates this perfectly: each failed batch cost little, allowing for repeated attempts with gradual improvement.

Remarkably, Taleb contends that "you don't have to be smart if you have convexity in your payoff." Success depends instead on sufficient rationality—avoiding repeated mistakes and adjusting variables between attempts.

## Career Applications

The convexity principle explains different optimal strategies across fields. Entrepreneurs benefit from making numerous low-cost bets, needing only one major success to offset losses. Software engineers, conversely, profit from building deep expertise incrementally over time.

Intelligence alone proves insufficient. The author observes that highly intelligent people sometimes fail because they repeat mistakes rather than learning from iterations. Rationality—the ability to adapt based on feedback—matters more than raw intellect.

## Conclusion

Trial and error deserves its favorable reputation, as demonstrated by historical figures like Roosevelt and physicist Richard Feynman. However, success requires favorable odds: ensure upsides exceed downsides, and no single failure eliminates future opportunities.
