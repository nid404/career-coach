# The Problems with Deliberate Practice
https://commoncog.com/the-problems-with-deliberate-practice/

By Cedric Chin

## Overview

Deliberate practice—the idea that specific types of practice lead to superior performance—has gained widespread popularity, particularly through Malcolm Gladwell's "10,000 hour rule." However, the research underlying this concept faces significant criticisms that deserve examination.

## Claim One: Talent is Overrated (But Isn't)

Ericsson's assertion that practice dominates talent is under heavy attack for several reasons:

**Inconsistent Definitions**: Ericsson has changed how he defines deliberate practice throughout his work, creating confusion about what precisely constitutes the concept.

**Overstated Universality Claims**: While Ericsson claims the principles apply to "anything," he simultaneously admits they don't work in domains like pop music or crossword puzzles—contradicting his broader assertions.

**Questionable Against Genetic Evidence**: The claim that practice matters more than genetics in expertise contradicts well-established findings showing genetic factors influence IQ, mental health, and other traits across virtually all human domains.

**Limited Falsifiability**: When people fail despite extensive practice, Ericsson dismisses these cases as "improper" deliberate practice—a circular argument that prevents meaningful testing.

Research by Hambrick and colleagues reveals striking variability: some chess players reached master level in under two years, while others needed 26 years despite identical practice amounts.

## Claim Two: Deliberate Practice Versus Normal Practice

For practitioners, the more relevant question is utility rather than pure truth. Deliberate practice does work but becomes tremendously difficult when applied to fields lacking established training methods.

Ericsson distinguishes between **purposeful practice** (focused, challenging, with feedback and specific goals) and **deliberate practice** (purposeful practice *plus* established training methods, coaching, and systematic skill building).

### Real-World Challenges

When working in domains without standardized training methods, practitioners face four major obstacles:

**Ill-Defined Subskills**: Learners must independently identify skill dependencies and perform their own skill extraction and syllabus design—themselves requiring specialized knowledge.

**Lack of Feedback**: Without a teacher, practitioners must creatively substitute feedback through methods like studying exemplary work, recording performances, or using spaced repetition software.

**Limited Practice Opportunities**: Real-world constraints often prevent sufficient repetition, requiring simulation techniques like decision-making exercises.

**Motivation Plateaus**: Without proper coaching to select appropriately-sized practice challenges in correct sequence, learners stagnate and lose motivation.

## Conclusion

Applying deliberate practice to career development is far more complex than popular interpretations suggest. Success requires understanding both the legitimate criticisms of the research and the practical difficulties of implementation in domains without established pedagogical traditions.
