# Paying Attention to Stories for Skill Extraction
https://commoncog.com/paying-attention-to-stories-for-skill-extraction/

By Cedric Chin

## Main Article

Our brains naturally store stories as valuable learning material. We retain narratives that seem relevant to our lives—just as ancient ancestors shared hunting tales by firelight, we save articles and remember anecdotes that might prove useful later.

What makes this process valuable is that stories often contain richer information than abstract principles alone. When a mentor provides general advice, you must figure out the context yourself. But when that same mentor illustrates a principle through multiple real-world examples, you gain the specific circumstances needed to apply that principle effectively.

## Why Stories Matter More Than Rules

Consider someone deciding whether to open a restaurant based on compliments about their home cooking. Anthony Bourdain's *Kitchen Confidential* provides crucial context: restaurants have enormous fixed costs (rent, utilities, insurance, licenses) and roughly an 80% failure rate. Most importantly, Bourdain captures the psychological reality—that panic sets in when your investment is hemorrhaging thousands monthly, forcing desperate changes that destroy your business.

Simply knowing "don't panic" differs vastly from understanding how panic actually manifests in a failing restaurant scenario.

## Extracting Actionable Knowledge from Narratives

When you encounter a valuable story, transform it into testable questions:

**From Bourdain's account, you might identify:**

- How long should you wait before concluding a restaurant concept isn't working?
- What constitutes adequate capitalization in your specific market?
- Which regulations govern food operations in your location?
- How does hiring actually work in hospitality?
- What inventory systems prevent excessive food waste?

For each question, ask yourself: (1) How confident am I in this insight? and (2) What's my strategy for finding answers?

## Building Your Skill-Development Plan

Rather than opening a restaurant blind, you could:

- Work inside existing successful restaurants at various levels to observe decision-making
- Interview experienced restaurateurs about their financial models
- Research local health and safety regulations
- Partner with industry veterans to review their operational costs

This transforms unknown unknowns into known unknowns—you now recognize what you don't know and can systematically pursue those answers before investing significant capital.

The author notes that nearly everything learned about running companies in Southeast Asia came through combining story-driven skill extraction with systematic experimentation and curious observation. Asking experienced mentors for narratives—rather than just abstract principles—reveals industry-wide patterns most newcomers never discover independently.
