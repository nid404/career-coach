## LAYER 1 — Worldview Synthesis

The underlying epistemology across this body of work is a rejection of the idea that reading and reasoning alone can reliably produce competence. The most important knowledge in any domain — the kind that actually produces results — is **tacit**: it lives in practice, not in propositions. You cannot hand it to someone through a framework, a blog post, or a mental model. It must be built through repeated contact with reality, and what gets built is specific to the person's situation, field, and learning history. This is what the Greeks called *technê*, and it is the load-bearing concept behind nearly every piece of advice here.

The corollary is that **reality is the final authority**. Not theory, not what sounds plausible, not even what scientists have proven at the population level — because group-level truths are often useless as prescriptions for an individual case. Evidence is evaluated in a hierarchy: what you've personally tested ranks above advice from a believable practitioner, which ranks above plausible argument. When you can't run an experiment directly, the proxy is the advisor's track record in that specific domain (Dalio's "believability": three relevant successes plus a coherent explanation of approach).

This worldview makes **trial and error a first-class method**, not a fallback. It works best when outcomes are convex — upside can be large, downside is capped and survivable — and when the person is rational enough to change variables between iterations rather than repeating the same mistake. Intelligence is not the scarce resource; learning efficiency is. The exceptional practitioner (Ng Teng Fong, not the average entrepreneur) extracts correct lessons from fewer cycles.

Two implications for evaluating advice: (1) **optimize for usefulness, not truth**. A framework that is technically accurate but doesn't change behavior is worth less than one that is slightly simplified but actionable. (2) **beware the simple story**. The brain generates and commits to the first plausible narrative about why something happened or what kind of person someone is. That narrative is almost always incomplete and often wrong. Generate multiple hypotheses; hold them simultaneously; let evidence update them rather than confirm them.

Skill development follows directly: **career capital is built through deliberate, effortful practice in the right direction**, not through time served or motivation. But deliberate practice as Ericsson defined it is only available in domains with established training methods — most knowledge-work domains don't have those, so the practitioner must do extra work to define subskills, create feedback mechanisms, and sequence challenges. Deep, focused attention is the prerequisite: the capacity to concentrate is itself scarce and trainable, and its opposite (shallow distraction) is the default drift of modern work.

Taken together: don't consume frameworks passively; test ideas against your own reality; weight sources by track record; build tacit skill through practice not reading; hold your stories about people and situations loosely; and stay solvent — never let a single failure eliminate future options.

### Career Moats 101
- **Source file:** career-moats-101.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Job security comes not from keeping your current role but from the ability to land the next one. A career moat — a competitive advantage in the job market — is built from rare and valuable skills: skills that standard education makes available but not scarce, so the moat requires going beyond credentials into specialization, unusual combinations, or early positioning in an emerging domain.
- **Named framework / concept:** Career moat (rare + valuable skills as competitive advantage); strategic inflection points (Andy Grove — monitor forces changing supplier/customer/competitor relationships before they're obvious).
- **When it applies:** Whenever evaluating whether a skill or role is building genuine long-term leverage; when assessing whether to stay on a current path or pivot; when diagnosing why someone feels replaceable or stuck.
- **Key distinctions:** Rare vs. valuable — a skill can be valuable (taught in every MBA) but not rare; both are required. Single exceptional skill vs. rare combination — two legitimate moat-building strategies with different risk profiles.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The article is a high-level overview and doesn't grapple with how long moat-building takes, how to know when you have one, or what to do when a moat erodes. Also doesn't address luck and timing — some inflection-point bets fail not from execution error but from misjudging the shift.

### So Good They Can't Ignore You
- **Source file:** so-good-they-cant-ignore-you.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** "Follow your passion" is backward: passion follows mastery, not the other way around. Build rare and valuable skills first (career capital), then trade them for autonomy and meaning. The craftsman mindset — obsessive focus on skill development — is the right posture for early-career.
- **Named framework / concept:** Career capital theory; craftsman mindset; winner-take-all vs. auction markets (two different skill-building strategies); Law of Financial Viability (Derek Sivers — only pursue autonomy if people will pay for your skills).
- **When it applies:** When someone is confused about direction and looking for "passion" as a guide; when diagnosing whether a job is worth staying in (does it offer skill development?); when thinking about how to build leverage for future autonomy.
- **Key distinctions:** Winner-take-all markets (one skill dominates — writing, performance) vs. auction markets (rare combinations rewarded). Autonomy trap: employers resist giving control precisely when you've built enough skill to deserve it — expect friction, not a smooth handoff.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The "mission" section of Newport's argument is weak — the examples don't survive scrutiny. The book also underplays luck and timing in career trajectories. The framework is most powerful for early-career skill accumulation; less useful as a guide for someone already competent figuring out what kind of work to do.

### Building Career Moats: A Confession
- **Source file:** building-career-moats-confession.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** Honest self-assessment of actual strengths and weaknesses — not aspirational ones — is the precondition for good career strategy. The author's moat came from identifying an underserved market (Southeast Asia operations), accepting a genuine weakness (algorithmic skills), and building toward a specific, unusual capability others wouldn't pursue.
- **Named framework / concept:** Dalio's three questions as a decision-making structure: (1) What do you know is true? (2) What are your goals? (3) What will you do given reality? Psychological pain as a signal of avoided truth.
- **When it applies:** When someone is telling a flattering story about their skills or trajectory; when a decision is being avoided; when "what should I do" is really "I don't want to look at what's true."
- **Limits / where it fails:** The author explicitly flags his own low believability — one moat, not three. The specific SEA play was also partly opportunity-of-the-moment; the principle (look for where others aren't going) is transferable, the specific execution was context-dependent.

### The Superiority of 'Trial & Error'
- **Source file:** trial-and-error.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Trial and error is a superior strategy — not just a fallback — when two conditions hold: randomness exists in outcomes, and the payoff structure is convex (upside potential exceeds downside cost of any single failure). Under convexity, rationality (learning from iterations) matters more than raw intelligence.
- **Named framework / concept:** Convexity of outcomes (Taleb) — the key condition determining when trial-and-error dominates planning. "You don't have to be smart if you have convexity in your payoff."
- **When it applies:** Evaluating whether to plan extensively vs. experiment; distinguishing domains where small bets make sense (entrepreneurship) from those where deep cumulative investment is better (specialist expertise); diagnosing why a smart person keeps failing (repeating mistakes vs. updating).
- **Key distinctions:** Convex vs. non-convex payoffs — the strategy only works if no single failure is eliminating. Rationality vs. intelligence — the former is what trial-and-error actually requires.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The framework doesn't tell you how to structure the experiments or extract the right lesson from a failure — that's the hard part. Convexity also isn't always easy to assess in advance; career bets that seem bounded can have long tail costs (reputation, relationship damage).

### Chinese Businessmen: Let Reality Be The Teacher
- **Source file:** reality-as-teacher.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** In fast-changing domains where knowledge is limited or unstable, trial-and-error dominates theory-first approaches. The exceptional practitioners (Ng Teng Fong, Robert Kuok) weren't just willing to iterate — they extracted correct lessons from failures faster than their peers. Learning efficiency, not intelligence, is the differentiating variable.
- **Named framework / concept:** "Trial and error never lies — it lets reality become the teacher." Theory-first vs. trial-and-error as two problem-solving modes, each appropriate to different knowledge conditions.
- **When it applies:** When someone is over-planning in an uncertain domain; when diagnosing a stall (are they failing to extract the right lesson?); as grounding for why advice must be personally tested rather than accepted at face value.
- **Key distinctions:** Theory-and-insight approach (structural engineering, where principles prevent failure) vs. trial-and-error (business, where things change too quickly for immutable rules). The distinction is domain-dependent — not a blanket endorsement of winging it.
- **Limits / where it fails:** Doesn't address the conditions under which trial-and-error is too costly (high failure cost, irreversible decisions, time pressure). Also the "learning efficiency" explanation for elite businessmen is a hypothesis, not demonstrated causally.

### Deep Work
- **Source file:** deep-work.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Focused, uninterrupted cognitive work is becoming simultaneously rarer and more economically valuable. The capacity for deep work is trainable, and its absence — caused by constant shallow distraction — is the default drift of modern professional life. Building deep work capacity is a competitive advantage, not just a productivity technique.
- **Named framework / concept:** Deep work vs. shallow work (Newport's core distinction); four depth philosophies (monastic, bimodal, rhythmic, journalistic); Four Disciplines of Execution applied to deep work; craftsman approach to tool selection (adopt only if benefits substantially outweigh costs).
- **When it applies:** When someone is busy but not advancing; when skill isn't improving despite time spent; when diagnosing the gap between output and effort; when evaluating how to structure a workday or block of time.
- **Key distinctions:** Deep vs. shallow — the line is roughly "could a smart new grad do this with minimal training?" Four depth philosophies differ in how much of the work context can be controlled — monastic requires near-total control, journalistic requires high trained capacity. Any-benefit approach vs. craftsman approach to tools — the former is too permissive.
- **Limits / where it fails:** Newport's framework assumes the individual has significant control over their time structure — consulting, client-service, or managed environments constrain this heavily. The willpower-as-muscle model underlying some techniques has weak replication. Maximum ~4 hours of deep work daily is a real ceiling that makes the techniques inapplicable to "more output" fantasies.

### Is the FIRE Movement a Good Idea?
- **Source file:** fire-movement.md
- **Type:** constraint/principle
- **Core claim:** FIRE (Financial Independence, Retire Early) is a fragile strategy if it replaces skill-building: a portfolio doesn't protect you in a transformed labor market if you've let your marketable skills atrophy. Financial prudence (frugality, early saving) is a sound foundation for career resilience — but only as a complement to, not substitute for, rare and valuable skills.
- **When it applies:** When evaluating the appeal of "exit" strategies vs. continued investment in skills; as a check against over-indexing on financial hedging at the expense of human capital; relevant specifically in high-automation-risk contexts.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The article is somewhat speculative — the catastrophic automation scenario is a worst-case. The geographic limitation (city-states vs. countries with hinterlands) is real but specific. The author's deeper point — that career capital and financial capital are complements, not substitutes — is the transferable principle.

### How to Read Self-Help
- **Source file:** how-to-read-self-help.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** Self-help is best treated as *technê* — craft knowledge requiring personal testing, not passive absorption. The correct posture is: source from believable practitioners, test through application, backtest against past experience, discard what doesn't hold. Reading self-help is justified because life is finite and we can't run all experiments personally.
- **Named framework / concept:** Self-help as technê; the Power Test (complex problems require powerful solutions — be skeptical of mindset tweaks as universal fixes); backtesting (does this advice explain past failures in a useful way?).
- **When it applies:** Whenever evaluating whether to adopt a new framework or practice; as a meta-heuristic governing how to treat any advice in the corpus itself.
- **Key distinctions:** Mindset change vs. structural change — "mindset changes only matter when combined with actual power to change your situation." Believable practitioners vs. self-proclaimed gurus — track record is the filter.
- **Limits / where it fails:** Backtesting is retrospective and subject to hindsight bias. The advice to seek "believable sources" assumes you can identify them — which is itself a judgment call that requires some prior credibility assessment. The framework is also somewhat circular: you need judgment to apply a framework designed to improve judgment.

### The Mental Model Fallacy
- **Source file:** mental-model-fallacy.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** The most valuable mental models — the ones that actually drive expert performance — cannot survive codification. What Warren Buffett "knows" about companies is not in his writings; it's in embodied pattern recognition built through decades of practice. Reading third-party accounts of mental models yields explicit knowledge but not tacit expertise, which is what actually differentiates performance.
- **Named framework / concept:** Explicit knowledge vs. tacit knowledge (technê); "experience precedes technical knowledge" (Gallwey) — you learn the technique by doing, then name it afterward, not the other way around.
- **When it applies:** Whenever someone is reading frameworks as a substitute for practice; when diagnosing why someone knows what to do but can't do it; as a check on the value of any distillation (including this one).
- **Key distinctions:** Non-practitioners writing about mental models vs. practitioners — the former write at the wrong resolution, lacking actionable handles. Knowing that "winner-take-all markets exist" vs. knowing how to compete within one — the gap is the tacit layer.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The argument can be used to dismiss all written knowledge, which goes too far — explicit knowledge is a scaffold that can accelerate tacit learning even if it can't replace it. The claim that "the most valuable models don't survive codification" is likely true but difficult to falsify.

### Teaching Tech Together
- **Source file:** teaching-tech-together.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Learning is mental model construction, not information absorption. Novices, competent practitioners, and experts differ not in how much they know but in how densely connected their knowledge graphs are. Expertise develops through deliberate practice with feedback — and self-feedback (the expert's own evaluation of their work) is ultimately more efficient than relying on external teachers.
- **Named framework / concept:** Novice/competent practitioner/expert model; knowledge as graph density; six evidence-based learning strategies (spaced practice, retrieval practice, interleaving, elaboration, concrete examples, dual coding); expert blind spot (experts can't remember not knowing, which makes them poor teachers).
- **When it applies:** When designing a practice regimen; diagnosing why learning isn't sticking; understanding why expertise is hard to transfer through advice; choosing how to allocate study time.
- **Key distinctions:** Retention vs. recall — recall ability limits long-term memory, not retention. Blocked practice vs. interleaving — blocked feels more effective but interleaving produces better long-term retention.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The learning strategies are well-evidenced in educational settings — their applicability to tacit skill development in professional domains is less clean. The novice/expert model is a simplification; real skill development is domain-specific and rarely linear.

### The Problems with Deliberate Practice
- **Source file:** problems-with-deliberate-practice.md
- **Type:** constraint/principle
- **Core claim:** Deliberate practice is a real and useful concept but its popular versions are oversimplified. The research has genuine weaknesses (genetic evidence is sidestepped, some definitional problems), and more importantly, deliberate practice as Ericsson defines it requires established training methods — which most knowledge-work domains don't have. In those domains, the practitioner must build their own curriculum, feedback mechanisms, and sequencing.
- **Named framework / concept:** Purposeful practice vs. deliberate practice (Ericsson's distinction): purposeful = focused, challenging, with feedback and specific goals; deliberate = purposeful + established training methods + coaching. Most people only have access to purposeful practice.
- **When it applies:** Whenever "just practice more" or "10,000 hours" is offered as advice; when diagnosing a stall in skill development; when designing a practice structure without access to expert coaching.
- **Key distinctions:** Purposeful vs. deliberate — the gap is the training infrastructure. Four obstacles in unstructured domains: ill-defined subskills, no feedback, limited practice opportunities, motivation plateaus.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The critique of genetic evidence is real but doesn't negate the practical utility of the framework — it just means results will vary more than Ericsson claims. Doesn't offer a fully satisfying answer to "what do you do when you can't access deliberate practice?" — that remains an open problem.

### Peak: The New Science of Expertise
- **Source file:** peak-book-summary.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Expertise is built through quality mental representations in long-term memory, developed progressively through purposeful practice. Naive practice (mere repetition) doesn't produce improvement and often entrenches bad habits. The goal of practice is to build better internal models, not to accumulate hours.
- **Named framework / concept:** Naive practice vs. purposeful practice vs. deliberate practice (the three-tier model); mental representations as the mechanism through which practice produces expertise.
- **When it applies:** When designing practice; when evaluating whether current effort is producing growth; as a complement to the "Problems with Deliberate Practice" piece for a full picture of Ericsson's framework.
- **Key distinctions:** "Practice makes permanent, not perfect" — naive repetition entrenches the current level. Purposeful practice: focused, uncomfortable, immediate feedback, specific goals. Deliberate practice: adds established training infrastructure.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The reviewer explicitly notes the book sidesteps genetics and doesn't solve practical implementation problems. Read alongside "Problems with Deliberate Practice" for an honest picture — *Peak* is an introduction, not a complete guide.

### Believability
- **Source file:** believability.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Weight advice and feedback by the advisor's demonstrated track record in the specific domain, not by their confidence, seniority, or general reputation. Believability = at least three relevant successes + coherent explanation of how they achieved them. With more believable people, ask questions; with less believable people, spend minimal time checking for overlooked blind spots, then move on.
- **Named framework / concept:** Believability heuristic (Dalio) — a triage tool for whose opinions to weight in decision-making.
- **When it applies:** Evaluating whether to accept feedback; deciding who to seek advice from; diagnosing when to hold a position vs. update; determining who to debate vs. question.
- **Key distinctions:** Domain-specificity is critical — a believable person in one domain is not automatically believable in another. Don't reflexively dismiss low-believability sources — briefly check for overlooked objections, then limit engagement.
- **Limits / where it fails:** Assessing believability itself requires judgment — the framework can be gamed or misapplied if the assessor has poor calibration. Three successes is also a fairly high bar that would disqualify most early-career advice-givers who may nonetheless have useful perspective. The framework is conservative by design.

### Optimise for Usefulness
- **Source file:** optimise-for-usefulness.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** Adopt beliefs and practices that advance your goals; replace those that don't. "Useful" is a different criterion from "true" — the question is whether a belief or mindset changes behavior in the right direction. This applies to how you interpret events, what lessons you extract from experience, and what heuristics you carry forward.
- **Named framework / concept:** Optimise for usefulness (the meta-principle); mindset tweaks as performance levers; the usefulness ladder (ascend from immediate reaction to transferable principle); distilling wrong lessons (the trap of premature, limiting conclusions).
- **When it applies:** When someone is stuck in an unhelpful frame; when evaluating what lesson to take from a difficult experience; when deciding what to believe about a situation where multiple interpretations are available.
- **Key distinctions:** Useful vs. true — not the same thing; a useful belief changes behavior, a true belief may not. High-usefulness vs. low-usefulness interpretation of the same event — the goal is to move up the ladder, not stay at the level of emotional reaction.
- **Limits / where it fails:** "Optimise for usefulness" can rationalize motivated reasoning — adopting the flattering interpretation rather than the useful one. The author's own hedge (Paul Graham: "if one theory gives you an excuse for being lazy, the other is probably right") is important but can be lost in application.

### A Personal Epistemology of Practice
- **Source file:** personal-epistemology-of-practice.md
- **Type:** worldview
- **Core claim:** Scientific truth and practical usefulness are different standards, and practitioners need the latter. Population-level correlations don't reliably predict individual outcomes. The right hierarchy for evaluating practical advice runs from personal testing (highest) through believable experts exposed to negative outcomes, down to plausible argument (lowest). Bayesian updating — adjusting confidence incrementally rather than accepting/rejecting wholesale — is the right posture when running personal experiments.
- **Named framework / concept:** Practitioner's hierarchy of evidence (five levels); Bayesian updating applied to practical advice; the science/usefulness gap ("scientists are interested in truth, but practitioners are interested in what is useful").
- **When it applies:** When deciding how much weight to give a piece of research, advice, or framework; when a personal experiment produces mixed or ambiguous results; when someone is treating scientific findings as direct prescriptions.
- **Key distinctions:** Scientific truth vs. practical usefulness — the population-level IQ/performance correlation (0.45-0.58) explains only 34% of variance and can't reliably prescribe individual action. Rejecting vs. downgrading — when something doesn't work, lower confidence rather than discard.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The framework requires enough self-knowledge to distinguish "this didn't work" from "I applied it wrong." Bayesian updating also demands good baseline calibration — if your priors are badly off, updating on noisy personal experiments can lead you further astray. The author also self-applies the believability check to himself (low believability at time of writing) — which the coach should note.

### Burnout: Symptoms, Causes, and Prevention
- **Source file:** burnout.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Burnout is three things simultaneously — exhaustion, cynicism, and ineffectiveness — not just tiredness. It is fundamentally an organizational/environmental mismatch, not a personal failure of resilience. Individual interventions (meditation, mindfulness) don't work while the person remains in the burnout-causing environment. Recovery requires removal and rest; prevention requires control over the work environment.
- **Named framework / concept:** Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) — the three-dimension definition; Job Demands-Resources model (JD-R); Areas of Worklife Scale (AWS) — six mismatch dimensions: workload, control, reward, community, fairness, values.
- **When it applies:** When someone reports chronic exhaustion, disengagement, or feeling ineffective; when evaluating whether a current situation is sustainable; when distinguishing "working hard" from "depleting without recovery."
- **Key distinctions:** Exhaustion alone ≠ burnout (all three dimensions required). Prevention vs. recovery — different interventions. Organizational problem vs. personal weakness — the research is clear that individual interventions fail without environmental change.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The AWS framework is useful for diagnosis but doesn't tell you which mismatch is most tractable to fix in a given role. In high-prestige, high-compensation environments (like consulting), the reward dimension can mask serious deficits in the other five — people stay longer than they should because the money or brand is good.

### Time Allocation as Capital Allocation
- **Source file:** time-allocation-as-capital-allocation.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Time allocation decisions can be thought about like capital allocation — each activity is a bet with probability of success and potential return. The Kelly Criterion's principles (don't overbet, require genuine edge, protect downside) translate loosely to career decisions: don't commit everything to one path, only pursue areas where you have real advantage, and avoid catastrophic failure to keep compounding options alive.
- **Named framework / concept:** Kelly Criterion applied to career time; downside protection as a prerequisite for compounding.
- **When it applies:** When evaluating how much to commit to a risky career bet; when someone is overcommitted to one path; when thinking about diversification vs. focus in skill development.
- **Limits / where it fails:** **The author has retracted this piece** (October 2023): his updated view is that capital is higher leverage than time, and investing ideas don't map cleanly to career decision-making. Use the intuitions cautiously — protect downside, require real edge — but don't apply the mathematical framework literally. The retraction is important: this is a case where the author's own believability test applies.

### A Better Way To Allocate Your Career Time
- **Source file:** better-way-to-allocate-career-time.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** When intuition fails (outside your circle of competence, high uncertainty), prioritize riskier tasks earlier — because failure on a dependent task early saves all the time you'd spend on downstream work. The "expected time saved" heuristic and failure-rate analysis are more reliable than gut feel for sequencing unfamiliar decisions.
- **Named framework / concept:** Expected time saved; failure-rate analysis (tackle highest λ = log(1/p)/T tasks first).
- **When it applies:** Sequencing tasks on an unfamiliar project; deciding which bet to test first in a new domain; any situation where human intuition about uncertainty is likely to be poor.
- **Limits / where it fails:** **The author has retracted this piece** (October 2023) with the same note as the time-allocation piece: capital leverage outweighs time considerations. The underlying intuition — test the riskiest assumption first — survives and is transferable. The formal framework should not be taken as a reliable decision-making tool.

### Paying Attention to Stories for Skill Extraction
- **Source file:** skill-extraction-from-stories.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Stories carry richer, more actionable knowledge than abstract principles because they embed context, conditions, and psychological reality. The method: when you hear a valuable story, convert it into specific testable questions (what don't I know? how confident am I? how would I find out?). This transforms unknown unknowns into known unknowns you can systematically pursue.
- **Named framework / concept:** Skill extraction via narrative; unknown unknowns → known unknowns as a learning progression.
- **When it applies:** When drawing lessons from a mentor's experience; when reading case studies or biographies; as a method for getting more from conversations with experienced practitioners; designing a skill-development plan in an unfamiliar domain.
- **Key distinctions:** Abstract principle vs. embedded story — "don't panic" differs entirely from Bourdain's account of how panic actually manifests in a failing restaurant, with monthly cash burn and desperate pivots. The story is what makes the principle operational.
- **Limits / where it fails:** Stories are also subject to narrative bias — the person telling them has usually shaped them to have a lesson. The extraction method helps but doesn't fully solve for the storyteller's own blind spots or motivated framing.

### Organisational Politics: Beware the Simple Story
- **Source file:** organisational-politics-simple-story.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** The brain generates the first plausible narrative about a person or event and then defends it through confirmation bias and belief persistence. This produces persistent misjudgments that constrain who you can work with and how accurately you read organizational dynamics. The corrective is to deliberately hold multiple competing narratives simultaneously and treat initial reads as hypotheses, not facts.
- **Named framework / concept:** Narrative machine / simple story (brain commits to first plausible explanation); fundamental attribution error (others' mistakes = character; own mistakes = circumstance); "discontinuous inconsistency" (the threshold required to update someone else's entrenched belief about you).
- **When it applies:** When forming an impression of a new manager or colleague; when someone seems to be locked in a negative pattern across multiple workplaces (likely self-generated); when you need to correct a misjudgment someone has formed about you; when a situation seems clearly one person's fault.
- **Key distinctions:** Small corrective actions vs. discontinuous inconsistency — if someone has a fixed story about you, small evidence gets dismissed; only surprising, difficult-to-explain evidence can break the frame.
- **Limits / where it fails:** "Generate multiple narratives" is cognitively demanding and competes with the need to act in fast-moving situations. Also doesn't fully address when the simple story is actually correct — not every harsh judgment is a bias, and the framework could be used to over-extend charity to people who are genuinely problematic.

### How to Ask Questions (So Your Boss Doesn't Hate You)
- **Source file:** how-to-ask-questions.md
- **Type:** framework
- **Core claim:** Question-asking is a learnable skill, and the critical failure mode is asking before showing your own work. Senior people resist mentoring those who seek hand-holding; they engage willingly with those who demonstrate they've genuinely tried, hit a real obstacle, and articulate specifically what they need. "Show your work" is the load-bearing step.
- **Named framework / concept:** Three-step structure: pick a good time → show your work → ask a well-formulated question. "Be someone that can be helped."
- **When it applies:** In any new role or unfamiliar workstream; when managing up; when building a mentorship relationship; when someone is getting poor responses to their questions or being labeled as high-maintenance.
- **Key distinctions:** "I can't do this, help me" vs. "I tried X and Y, here's why they failed, should I try Z?" — the latter is not just more palatable, it actually produces better answers because it surfaces the specific gap.
- **Limits / where it fails:** The framework works in environments where effort is valued and legible. In some organizational cultures, showing work is read as lack of confidence rather than thoroughness — calibration to the specific manager matters. Also, the technique is less useful when you're so unfamiliar with a domain you don't know what you've tried is even the right category of approach.
