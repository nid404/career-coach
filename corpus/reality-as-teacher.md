# Chinese Businessmen: Let Reality Be The Teacher
https://commoncog.com/chinese-businessmen-let-reality-be-the-teacher/

By Cedric Chin

## The Traditional Path: Learning by Doing

The first generation of overseas Chinese businessmen built their expertise through practical experience rather than formal education. Two generations ago, completing primary school was exceptional, so business knowledge came from hands-on experience starting in childhood.

Singapore's Ng Teng Fong exemplifies this approach. He never attended school but built Far East Organisation into one of Asia's premier real estate developers. After his parents' modest soy sauce factory and grocery shop, Ng launched his own provision shop—which failed catastrophically. He reportedly became so impoverished he had to give away one of his eight children. Yet he persisted, eventually achieving success in real estate development over subsequent decades.

Ng extended this learning methodology to his son Robert, sending him to Hong Kong at age 22 to navigate business independently. As George Yeo observed, Robert "learnt quickly to hold his own against the big Hong Kong players."

## Two Problem-Solving Approaches

Education systems prioritize "theory and insight"—the approach used in fields like structural engineering where understanding underlying principles prevents failure. However, this bias toward careful deliberation doesn't serve all domains equally.

Business represents a different challenge: "things change too quickly for there to be immutable rules." When knowledge is limited or volatile, trial-and-error becomes superior. Rather than extensive planning, successful entrepreneurs test ideas, learn from failures, and adapt rapidly.

## The Excellence Question

What distinguishes exceptional businessmen like Ng Teng Fong, Robert Kuok, or Mochtar Riady from merely successful ones? The author theorizes they excel at learning efficiency—extracting correct lessons from mistakes using fewer cycles.

Negative examples prove illuminating: entrepreneurs who repeatedly fail to recognize distribution problems or staff management issues cannot extract appropriate lessons from their failures. They appear "bizarre" because they repeat identical mistakes expecting different outcomes.

## The Real Advantage

When failure costs remain low, testing remains affordable, and knowledge gaps persist, trial-and-error dominates as the optimal strategy. This approach worked for traditional Chinese businessmen navigating unfamiliar foreign markets and cultures.

The key distinction: "trial and error never lies. It lets reality become the teacher."
