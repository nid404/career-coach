## Overview

This is a summary of Greg Wilson's *Teaching Tech Together*, focusing on chapters about how humans learn and acquire knowledge. Rather than covering the book's full scope on teaching programming, this article emphasizes learning science principles applicable to self-directed learners.

## How Humans Learn

### Mental Models and Knowledge Construction

Wilson emphasizes that learning occurs through constructing mental models rather than passively absorbing information. As Seymour Papert's work suggests, humans build knowledge iteratively, replacing flawed models with improved ones.

The key insight: "The way you teach novices is to help them construct the right mental models, so they have somewhere to put your facts." This explains why simple explanations often fail—learners need examples and varied approaches until concepts click.

### Three Levels of Competence

- **Novices**: Lack usable mental models and don't know what they don't know
- **Competent practitioners**: Possess functional models sufficient for routine tasks
- **Experts**: Hold densely interconnected mental models handling complex situations and edge cases

## Mental Models as Graphs

Knowledge can be visualized as networks where nodes represent facts and connections show relationships:

- Novices start with sparse, often incorrect connections
- Competent practitioners develop basic organizational frameworks
- Experts build densely connected networks enabling rapid problem-solving

This density explains expert "intuition"—direct mental pathways from problems to solutions—and the "expert blind spot," where specialists struggle to teach because they cannot remember not understanding their field.

## Concept Maps as Learning Tools

Concept maps externalize thinking by visualizing knowledge connections. Teachers use them for lesson planning, real-time instruction, and student assessment. They function as collaborative tools best when appearing rough and unfinished, encouraging honest feedback.

## Working Memory Constraints

Human short-term memory holds approximately 7±2 discrete items. This limitation means:

- Teaching should present at most 7±2 concepts per session
- Information must transfer to long-term memory through rehearsal
- Complex lessons should be split into multiple parts

## Six Evidence-Based Learning Strategies

### 1. Spaced Practice
Distributing study across multiple days proves more effective than concentrated sessions. Create flashcards for forgotten material to focus subsequent reviews.

### 2. Retrieval Practice
Recall ability, not retention, limits long-term memory. Testing yourself through exercises, summarization, or flashcards strengthens retrieval pathways.

### 3. Interleaving
Mixing different topics during study (A-B-C-B-A-C) outperforms blocked practice (A-A-B-B-C-C), creating more cross-topic connections.

### 4. Elaboration
Self-explanation while studying improves understanding. Explicitly discuss why answers are correct or why alternatives fail.

### 5. Concrete Examples
Generating specific instances of abstract principles strengthens learning. The ADEPT method structures this: provide **A**nalogy, **D**iagram, **E**xample, **P**lain language, and **T**echnical details.

### 6. Dual Coding
Combining complementary text and visuals leverages separate brain processing systems. Pairing diagrams with narratives or unlabeled illustrations with retrieval practice enhances learning.

## Expertise Development

Becoming expert requires deliberate practice: performing similar yet varied tasks, monitoring results, and adjusting behavior based on feedback. Progression typically moves from receiving external feedback to giving it to others to self-feedback, with the last stage proving most efficient due to faster feedback loops.

The article notes this represents a comprehensive framework rather than novel techniques, with convergence across cognitive and behavioral psychology suggesting these principles reflect fundamental truths about human learning.
