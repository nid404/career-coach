# Deep Work
https://commoncog.com/deep-work/

By Cedric Chin

## Overview

Cal Newport's 2016 book *Deep Work* is a handbook for maintaining focus in our hyperconnected world. It's divided into two parts: the first establishes why deep work matters for career advancement, and the second provides practical techniques for implementing it.

## Part One: Why Deep Work Matters

### Definition

Newport distinguishes between two types of professional activity:

**Deep Work:** Activities performed with full concentration that push cognitive abilities to their limits, create new value, improve skills, and are difficult to replicate.

**Shallow Work:** Tasks requiring minimal cognitive effort, typically performed while distracted, that create little value and are easily replicated.

### Three Arguments for Deep Work's Importance

**1. Economic Bifurcation**

The labor market is dividing between winners and losers due to technological advancement. Three types of workers will thrive:

- **Skilled Workers:** Those who can extract valuable results from complex machines (example: statistician Nate Silver)
- **Superstars:** The best practitioners in their fields, competing globally (example: programmer David Heinemeier Hansson)
- **Capital Owners:** Venture capitalists and investors positioned to benefit from machine productivity

Developing rare, valuable skills through deep work is the path to becoming either a skilled worker or superstar.

**2. Deep Work is Rare**

Modern workplaces drift toward shallow activities—open offices, constant messaging, social media presence—because they're easier and create visible signs of busyness. Those who resist this trend gain competitive advantage. Newport notes that "in the absence of clear indicators of what it means to be productive" many workers demonstrate busyness rather than output.

**3. Deep Work Supports a Meaningful Life**

Three perspectives support this claim:

- **Neurological:** Attention management directly influences well-being. Shallow work trains our brains to chase stimulation, while deep work develops our ability to focus on what matters.

- **Psychological:** Mihaly Csikszentmihaly's research on flow states shows that immersion in focused activity is inherently rewarding, regardless of the task.

- **Philosophical:** Following Dreyfus and Kelly, craftsmanship—the deliberate cultivation of skill—provides meaning without requiring glamorous work. The meaning comes from the practice itself, not the job title.

## Part Two: Techniques for Achieving Deep Work

### Rule 1: Work Deeply

#### 1.1 Choose Your Depth Philosophy

Select an approach matching your circumstances:

- **Monastic:** Complete isolation from shallow obligations (Donald Knuth, Neal Stephenson). Requires a singular, highly valued professional goal.

- **Bimodal:** Alternate between periods of deep isolation and normal activity (Adam Grant divides teaching and research by semester).

- **Rhythmic:** Defend specific daily times for deep work (Jerry Seinfeld's chain method).

- **Journalistic:** Fit deep work into available gaps (Walter Isaacson). Requires confidence and practiced skill.

#### 1.2 Ritualize Your Practice

Successful creators "think like artists but work like accountants." Establish rituals addressing:

- **Location and duration:** Choose environments supporting productivity and set fixed session lengths.
- **Rules and processes:** Create structure eliminating constant self-regulation.
- **Support systems:** Ensure adequate coffee, food, or exercise as needed.

#### 1.3 Make Grand Gestures

Significant commitment signals importance. J.K. Rowling's hotel retreat to finish her novel, Bill Gates's cabin retreats, and Alan Lightman's summer island retreats all worked because they psychologically prioritized the task, unlocking necessary mental resources. The gesture need not be expensive—merely substantial enough to signal seriousness.

#### 1.4 Balance Collaboration and Solitude

Separate activities seeking serendipitous collaboration from deep work sessions. When collaborating, seek relationships that deepen both parties' thinking—what Newport calls the "whiteboard effect," where experimentalist and theorist push each other to greater depths.

MIT's Building 20 exemplified this principle: its replacement building preserved soundproofed offices within common areas, creating a hub-and-spoke architecture balancing isolation with chance encounters.

#### 1.5 Execute Like a Business

Apply the "Four Disciplines of Execution" framework:

- **Focus on the Wildly Important:** Identify a small number of ambitious outcomes (Newport's example: "publish five papers this year").

- **Act on Lead Measures:** Track hours spent in deep work rather than distant outcomes like tenure or promotion.

- **Keep a Compelling Scoreboard:** Display deep work hours visibly. Newport taped a card by his monitor with weekly rows and tick marks, circling marks corresponding to completed papers.

- **Create Accountability:** Hold weekly reviews examining previous performance and planning ahead.

#### 1.6 Schedule Downtime

Counterintuitively, regular breaks improve deep work capacity:

- Downtime aids insight by allowing subconscious problem-solving.
- Involuntary attention during walks or nature experiences restores directed attention capacity.
- Evening work rarely contributes meaningfully to career goals.

Research shows maximum deliberate practice capacity is roughly four hours daily. Newport recommends a shutdown ritual:

1. Review your email for urgent items.
2. Transfer new tasks to your todo list.
3. Skim all tasks and review upcoming calendar items.
4. Plan tomorrow roughly.
5. Say "Shutdown complete."

This ritual combats the Zeigarnik effect (incomplete tasks dominating attention) by creating a completion plan, freeing evening mental resources.

### Rule 2: Embrace Boredom

Constant digital distraction rewires brains to crave novelty. Clifford Nass's research shows that brains accustomed to on-demand distraction struggle with concentration even when attempting focus.

#### 2.1 Take Breaks From Focus, Not Distraction

Reverse the common approach: keep internet access off by default, and schedule specific online blocks. This builds resistance to stimulation-seeking when facing boredom or cognitive challenge.

Key principles:
- Schedule internet use in advance and strictly avoid it elsewhere.
- Note the next permitted internet time to resist temptation.
- If urgent information is needed during offline blocks, wait five minutes before going online to maintain training integrity.
- Apply this discipline both at work and home.

#### 2.2 Work Like Teddy Roosevelt

Set artificially tight deadlines at the edge of feasibility. Extreme time pressure naturally eliminates distractions and forces concentration. Practice this no more than weekly initially, building capacity gradually.

#### 2.3 Productive Meditation

Focus on professional problems during mindless physical activity (walking, lawn mowing, waiting). Structure your thinking:

- Identify variables and store them mentally.
- Define your specific next-step question.
- Summarize findings before moving forward.
- Gently redirect attention when your mind wanders to unrelated or previously-solved problems.

#### 2.4 Memorize a Deck of Cards

Engage in any structured thinking requiring unwavering attention. Beyond the activity itself, these practices significantly increase general concentration capacity. Daniel Kilov transformed from a struggling student with diagnosed attention deficit disorder to graduating with first-class honors through memory training.

### Rule 3: Quit Social Media

This rule advocates evaluating social media use rationally rather than abandoning it entirely.

**The Problem:** Most people use the "Any-Benefit Approach," justifying tools by identifying any possible benefit. This ignores substantial costs to attention.

**The Solution:** Adopt the "Craftsman Approach to Tool Selection":

Identify factors determining professional and personal success. Adopt tools only if positive impacts substantially outweigh negative impacts.

**The 30-Day Test:**

Stop using all social media for 30 days without announcing it or deleting accounts. Then evaluate:

1. What are your core professional and personal goals?
2. What activities best contribute to each goal?
3. Do your social media tools substantially help or harm participation in these activities?

Newport emphasizes not announcing your departure because social networks rely on reciprocity psychology—the implicit agreement that you'll return attention received. Disappearing tests whether your participation actually matters.

Ultimately, Newport suggests these services are "products, developed by private companies, funded lavishly, marketed carefully, and designed ultimately to capture then sell your personal information and attention to advertisers," not the essential infrastructure they're marketed as.

### Rule 4: Drain the Shallows

Shallow work naturally expands to fill available time, so deliberate reduction techniques are necessary.

#### 4.1 Schedule Everything

At each workday's start, create an hour-by-hour schedule on lined paper. Divide work hours into blocks, assigning specific activities.

- Minimum block length is 30 minutes.
- You won't stick perfectly to schedules—that's fine. Spend five minutes readjusting when changes occur.
- Throw out schedules when important insights emerge.
- Use conditional overflow blocks for tasks potentially exceeding estimates.
- Build generous task block durations to accommodate surprises.

Over time, you'll improve at estimating task duration while remaining mindful of how you spend time.

#### 4.2 Create a Shallow Work Budget

Define shallow work as: work that could be completed by a smart recent graduate with no specialized training in your field within a reasonable timeframe.

Aim for approximately four hours of deep work daily, allocating remaining time for shallow work. However, shallow work tends to expand, so actively maintain boundaries.

#### 4.3 Cap Your Day

Newport ends work at 5:30 PM daily, creating artificial scarcity that forces ruthless prioritization. The Pareto Principle suggests only vital activities directly contribute to success. A work cap acts as a forcing function, concentrating effort on tenure-advancing activities.

This "scarcity mindset" raises the bar for claiming your time and attention.

#### 4.4 Become Hard to Reach

Email consumes substantial time. Three tactics reduce dependence:

- **Make senders work harder:** Use contact forms explaining your time constraints and conditions for response.

- **Do more work when responding:** Use process-oriented replies. Instead of "What time works?" write: "Let's meet at Starbucks. I'm free Tuesday at 2, 3, or 4 PM, or Wednesday at 1, 2, or 3 PM. Reply confirming a slot or call if none work." This shortcuts lengthy email chains.

- **Don't respond:** The sender bears responsibility for demonstrating that a reply is worthwhile. Non-critical emails can simply go unanswered.

## Conclusion

Newport's techniques rest on a foundation often questioned—willpower research has faced replication challenges. However, the methods remain valuable as practical tools that work regardless of underlying theory.

Deep work is achievable through deliberate philosophy selection, structured rituals, attention discipline, social media evaluation, and shallow work reduction. The payoff—both professionally and psychologically—makes the effort substantial.
