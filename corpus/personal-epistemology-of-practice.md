# Putting Mental Models to Practice Part 6: A Personal Epistemology of Practice

## Introduction

The article addresses a fundamental question: how do you evaluate advice before implementing it in practice? The author argues that most people rely on intuition—"I trust my gut"—but proposes a more rigorous framework grounded in epistemology (the study of knowledge and truth).

## The Problem with Scientific Knowledge

While scientific research represents the "gold standard" for truth, the author identifies critical limitations when applying it to personal practice:

**Statistical Predictors Don't Work at Individual Level**

The correlation between IQ and job performance (0.45-0.58) illustrates this problem. As the author explains, "Scientists are interested in truth, but practitioners are interested in what is useful." A correlation that explains only 34% of variance can't reliably predict individual performance, despite being scientifically valid.

**Science Focuses on General Truths, Not Prescriptive Solutions**

The author notes that "most studies don't have the sorts of prescriptions that are specifically useful to you in your field." Research on burnout, for instance, has produced detection tests but not rigorous prevention systems after two decades of study.

## A Hierarchy of Evidence for Practitioners

The author proposes an alternative pyramid for evaluating practical advice:

1. **Advice you've tested yourself** (highest)
2. **Believable experts exposed to negative outcomes** (using Ray Dalio's "believability" framework: three successes plus credible explanations)
3. **Merely believable experts**
4. **Practitioners who've attempted the advice**
5. **Plausible arguments** (lowest)

### On Credibility and Ad Hominem

The author defends considering the source: "Credibility counts when it comes to practical matters." This isn't fallacious reasoning—it's pragmatic. With limited time, weighting advice by the advisor's track record is rational.

## Managing Confounding Variables

Since practical experimentation lacks scientific rigor, confounding variables complicate interpretation. These include genetics, cultural environment, prerequisite skills, context differences, and external advantages.

The solution involves **Bayesian updating**: assign confidence ratings to advice and adjust them through testing. When Cal Newport's "breaks from focus" technique didn't work for the author, rather than dismissing it entirely, they lowered their confidence rating and considered which variables might explain the difference—genetics, prerequisite habits, or environmental factors.

## Core Framework Summary

The author distills the epistemology into principles:

- Let reality be the teacher for both scientific and anecdotal evidence
- Remember that scientific truth differs from practical usefulness
- Weight advice according to a practitioner-focused hierarchy
- Use Bayesian updating when testing claims against personal experience

## Meta-Observation

The author demonstrates intellectual honesty by applying their own standards to their work. They're not "believable" by Dalio's criteria (only two business successes under 50 people each), so readers shouldn't accept the framework on rhetoric alone—only through personal testing.

The article concludes with Bruce Lee's philosophy: absorb what is useful, discard what isn't, and add what's uniquely yours.
