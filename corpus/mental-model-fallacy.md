# The Mental Model Fallacy
https://commoncog.com/the-mental-model-fallacy/

By Cedric Chin

## Main Article

The core argument presented is that reading descriptions of mental models written by non-practitioners is largely ineffective for achieving success. The author uses an MMA analogy to illustrate: listening to commentators describe fighting techniques won't make you a champion—you need actual practice.

The author distinguishes between two types of knowledge:

**Explicit knowledge** consists of codifiable frameworks and facts—things that can be written down. Examples include understanding that "winner-take-all markets exist" or how economic cycles work.

**Tacit knowledge** (or *technê*) cannot be adequately expressed through words alone. It represents embodied skill, intuition, and the "feeling" of executing actions correctly. This tacit dimension is what truly distinguishes experts from non-experts.

The central claim: "The most valuable mental models do not survive codification. They cannot be expressed through words alone."

The author argues that successful practitioners like Warren Buffett possess mental models that function as internalized navigation systems—they "see an embodied model of a company" rather than consulting mental checklists. No amount of reading codified mental models can replicate this embodied expertise.

## The Problem with Third-Party Accounts

Non-practitioners writing about mental models lack the contextual experience needed to communicate them effectively. They write at the wrong resolution, lacking actionable handles and practical guidance. A blog post about winner-take-all markets, for instance, may describe the concept but fails to answer: what strategies should *you* employ if operating within one?

## Learning Tacit Knowledge

Drawing on Timothy Gallwey's *The Inner Game of Tennis*, the author demonstrates that learning physical skills relies on imitation and embodied practice rather than verbal instruction. Excessive explanation actually impairs performance. The book reveals that "experience precedes technical knowledge"—techniques emerge from lived experience, and "words can only represent actions."

This principle extends beyond sports to software design, visual design, and business management.

## Recommendation

Instead of reading aggregated mental model blogs, practitioners should study source materials directly from experts in their field, copy their actions, develop their own tacit knowledge through deliberate practice, and reflect through trial and error.
